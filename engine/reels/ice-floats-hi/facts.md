# Why ice floats

## The one-line answer
Water molecules lock into an open hexagonal cage when they freeze, which spreads them out about 9% in volume, so ice is lighter than the water around it and floats.

## The myth
"Everything gets denser as it gets colder, so ice should sink." True for almost every other liquid, but water breaks the rule: it is densest at about 4 °C, then gets lighter as it cools to 0 °C and lighter again as it freezes. [1][2] (This framing of the intuition is ours; the physics is sourced. A second common slip: hydrogen bonds do not "push" molecules apart; they pull, and the cage they build is simply roomy. Not source-checked, so not used as a fact.)

## Facts
1. Ordinary ice (ice Ih) has density 0.9167 g/cm³ at 0 °C; liquid water at 0 °C is 0.99984 g/cm³. — [3][4] (High: two sources agree on 0.9167)
2. Freezing lowers density by about 9%, i.e. the water expands about 9% in volume (0.99984 / 0.9167 = 1.091, our arithmetic from [3][4]). — [2][4][5] (High: three sources agree)
3. Liquid water is densest at 3.98 °C (0.99997 g/cm³), often rounded to 4 °C. It is less dense both warmer and colder than that. — [4][6] (High: two sources agree)
4. In ice Ih every oxygen has four neighbours at roughly tetrahedral angles (near 109.5°; a free water molecule's bend is about 104.5°). Each bond carries one hydrogen. — [3][4] (High)
5. The lattice is made of tessellating hexagonal rings in stacked ABAB layers. — [3] (Medium: one source read)
6. The oxygen-to-oxygen distance along a bond is about 275 pm in ice, with the hydrogen sitting about 101 pm from its own oxygen. The remaining ~174 pm to the neighbour oxygen is our subtraction. — [3] (Medium: one source; the subtraction is arithmetic, not a measured hydrogen-bond length)
7. The O–O distance is longer in ice than in liquid water, which is why ice is less dense. — [7] (Medium: one research paper, qualitative, no numbers given)
8. Hydrogen bonds are weak next to the covalent O–H bonds, and in liquid water they break and re-form faster than 200 femtoseconds. — [4] (Medium: one source)
9. In a cooling lake, surface water sinks until the whole lake is near 4 °C. After that, colder surface water is lighter and stays on top, so ice forms at the surface. — [2][6] (High: two sources agree)
10. Floating ice insulates the water below, so ponds do not usually freeze solid and fish can survive winter. — [2] (Medium: one source)
11. Salt shifts things: average seawater (35 ‰) is 1.028 g/cm³ at 4 °C; salinity lowers the temperature of maximum density. — [2] (Medium: one source; the other source gives 1024 to 1028 kg/m³ for seawater [6], so they agree)
12. Typically about one-tenth of an iceberg is above water; pure ice is about 920 kg/m³ and seawater about 1,025 kg/m³. — [8] (Medium: one source. Our check: 1 − 917/1025 = 10.5% above, 89.5% below, consistent. Real icebergs hold air bubbles, so it varies.)
