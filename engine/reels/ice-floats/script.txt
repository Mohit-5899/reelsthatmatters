Cold water sinks. So why does frozen water float on top?

Watch a lake in autumn. The surface water cools, gets heavier, and sinks. That goes on until the whole lake is near four degrees.

Then the rule flips. Below four degrees, water gets lighter as it cools. The coldest water stays on top, and that is where the ice forms.

To see why, zoom in. In liquid water, the molecules tumble past each other. The weak bonds between them break and re-form in a fraction of a trillionth of a second, so nothing holds still.

Freeze them, and every oxygen locks onto four neighbours. The bonds join into rings of six, stacked layer on layer.

That cage is roomy. The molecules sit farther apart than they did in the liquid, so the same water takes up about nine percent more space.

Same water, more room. Ice ends up lighter than the water around it, so it rises. That floating lid insulates the water below, so ponds usually don't freeze solid.

Ice floats because freezing opens water into a cage that is lighter than the water it came from.
