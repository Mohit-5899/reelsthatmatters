# Why ice floats: glass + lake in one world, molecules in a second world, back to the glass for the payoff.
== hook | ground: dark
Cold water *sinks.*
So why does *frozen* water / float on top?
== lake | ground: dark
Watch a *lake* in autumn.
The surface water cools, / gets heavier, and *sinks.*
That goes on until / the whole lake is
near *four* degrees.
Then the rule *flips.*
Below four degrees,
water gets *lighter* as it cools.
The coldest water stays on top,
and that is where / the *ice* forms.
== liquid | ground: dark
To see why, *zoom* in.
In liquid water,
the molecules *tumble* / past each other.
The weak bonds between them
break and re-form in a fraction
of a *trillionth* of a second,
so nothing holds *still.*
== cage | ground: dark
Freeze them, and every / *oxygen* locks onto
four neighbours.
The bonds join into / *rings* of six,
stacked layer on layer.
== roomy | ground: dark
That *cage* is roomy.
The molecules sit *farther* apart
than they did in the liquid,
so the same water takes up
about nine *percent* more space.
== payoff | ground: dark
Same water, more *room.*
Ice ends up *lighter* than
the water around it, so it rises.
That floating *lid* insulates
the water below, so ponds
usually don't freeze *solid.*
Ice floats because freezing
opens water into a *cage*
that is lighter than the water
it came from.
